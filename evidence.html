What students actually produce

Student Work

The test of a pedagogy is what it produces. These are real artifacts from over 500 first-year students from 70+ countries in my EAP courses at NYU Shanghai since 2018. Each example below describes the assignment, what students do, and what it demonstrates.

01 

Reflective practice

Reflection is the engine of the Students as Partners approach. Students use the DEIP framework (Describe, Evaluate, Interpret, Plan) to analyze their own learning, group dynamics, and community engagement. These are not afterthoughts: they are assessed, scaffolded, and iterative.

Reflective journal

Volunteering with autistic children at a charity shop

A first-year student reflects on an experiential learning placement at a Shanghai charity shop serving young adults with autism. Using the four-stage DEIP cycle, the student moves from describing the experience through evaluating their own assumptions, interpreting the encounter through course concepts about inclusion and social responsibility, and planning what they would do differently next time.

What it demonstrates: the capacity to connect personal experience with academic frameworks, genuine engagement with disability and inclusion, and evidence of growth across the semester.

Includes photo evidence from the placement. Written as a weekly journal entry in the student's own voice.

Reflective journal framework

The DEIP writing scaffold

The structured framework students use each week: Describe what happened, Evaluate what went well or poorly, Interpret what it means using course concepts, Plan what to change. Every student completes this cycle weekly for their personal learning journal and their academic journal.

Why it matters: reflection is not left to chance. Students who have never written reflectively before are given a replicable structure that builds metacognitive habits across the semester.
Active learning

Academic listening at the Shanghai Musical Theater Festival

A student applies active listening and presentation analysis skills from class to a real-world event: the 4th SHCM International Musical Theater Festival. The assignment asks students to attend any public event and use the analytical listening framework taught in class to produce a structured response.

What it demonstrates: transfer of academic skills beyond the classroom. Students prove they can use analytical frameworks independently, without prompting, in contexts they choose themselves.
Self-directed learning

Student-designed learning goals with peer evaluation

Students set their own learning goals for the semester, track their progress in writing, evaluate their own and their peers' contributions to group projects, and plan their next steps. The instructor reviews but does not set the goals: students own the entire cycle.

SaP in action: students hold genuine decision-making power over what they learn and how they measure it. Peer evaluation is not anonymous feedback: it is a structured conversation.
02 

Research and communication

Academic English is not grammar drills. It is the ability to find credible sources, synthesize information, take a position, and defend it in front of an audience. Every assignment in these courses builds toward that.

Research report

SDG research with primary and secondary sources

Each student chooses a Sustainable Development Goal, conducts original research using primary sources (interviews, surveys, campus data) alongside secondary academic sources, and produces a structured report with data analysis, evidence-based argumentation, and policy recommendations.

What it demonstrates: the full research cycle, from question to data to argument to recommendation, in a student's first semester of university. Many students later cite this as the first real research they have done.
Campaign planning

Team-designed SDG experiential learning campaign

Student teams negotiate roles, set campaign goals, plan logistics for an on-campus sustainability campaign, and run the event. The planning worksheet captures the negotiation process: who does what, why, and what happens when someone does not deliver. Peer review and self-evaluation are built into every stage.

What it demonstrates: collaboration under real constraints. Students do not just discuss teamwork in the abstract: they experience friction, resolve it, and document what they learned from the process.
03 

Community engagement and leadership

Learning does not stop at the classroom door. These artifacts show students connecting academic skills to community life, cross-cultural events, and institutional leadership.

Through a sustained partnership with Stepping Stones Shanghai (initiated 2016 at Harrow, continued through 2025 at NYU Shanghai), students volunteer teach English to migrant and underserved children. The (Un)Sustainability course also partners with Good-hope Bakery of Special Youth, a public welfare bakery providing supportive employment for young adults with neurodiversity. Both partnerships embed community service directly into assessed coursework, not as an add-on.

Service and leadership

LEAD Ambassadors promoting cross-cultural understanding

LEAD mentorship students designed and ran a tabling event for the “No Need For Translation” cross-cultural initiative. The documentation captures event planning, outreach materials created by students, photos of the event, and a reflection on what worked and what they would change.

What it demonstrates: student leadership in action. These are first-generation and underrepresented students who arrived at university months earlier, now running public-facing events that shape campus culture.
Participation tracking

Collaborative semester-long engagement tracker

A shared spreadsheet where each student logs their on-campus, in-class, and off-campus engagement throughout the semester. The tracker is not a surveillance tool: it is used collaboratively between student and instructor during one-on-one meetings to discuss participation grades, identify patterns, and set goals.

SaP in action: grades are not imposed. They are discussed, with evidence, in a conversation where the student's own record is the starting point. Students who disagree with a participation assessment can point to specific entries.
