The Practice

I build curriculum. I build systems. I build vision. I build policy that connects to the populations it is written for.

Leadership & vision

In November 2025 I was accepted into the National Professional Qualification for Headship (NPQH) at the UCL (University College London) Institute of Education. My implementation project, AI and Academic Integrity, examines how school leaders can guide their communities through the challenges of generative AI: not with fear or blanket bans, but with ethical frameworks, redesigned assessments, and genuine collaboration with teachers and students.

In August 2025 I passed the School Leaders Licensure Assessment (SLLA 6990) with a score of 164, the assessment many US states use in licensing school administrators. I also hold a current Vermont English Language Arts Teaching License, Grades 7–12 (valid through June 2029).

Leadership diagnostics through the NPQH consistently identify moral purpose, authenticity, composure under pressure, and decisiveness as my core strengths. What I am deliberately building is patience with pace: bringing a whole staff along rather than moving at the speed of the people who already agree with me.

My vision for education: schools and universities that are ethical, evidence-informed learning communities where young people grow as whole people, intellectually, socially, and morally, rather than places that simply produce exam results.

02 

AI governance in practice

Policy only works when the people living with it help shape it. My AI work sits in that gap between what a policy says and what actually happens in a classroom.

•NPQH implementation project, UCL Institute of Education: AI and Academic Integrity, guiding a school community through generative AI with ethical frameworks, redesigned assessment, and real collaboration with teachers and students.
•Policy grounded in frameworks: built on the DEC AI Literacy Framework and ethical frameworks for evaluating generative AI tools.
•Evidence base: International AI Safety Report 2026 (Extended Summary for Policymakers, February 2026), the independent scientific assessment written by 100+ experts nominated by 30+ countries, the EU, OECD, and UN. I use it to ground AI decisions in evidence rather than hype or fear. Four findings shape my work directly:
  • Detection doesn't work. People mistook AI-generated text for human writing 77% of the time. Integrity policy can't rest on spotting AI, so I redesign assessment instead.
  • AI underserves less-represented languages and cultures. One study found 79% accuracy on US cultural questions and 12% on Ethiopian ones. That matters for multilingual students who are told to trust these tools.
  • Over-reliance erodes skill. Users accept wrong AI suggestions when checking takes effort, and skills can decline with sustained AI assistance. Assessment has to protect the struggle where learning happens.
  • No single safeguard is enough. The report's defence-in-depth model is how I build school AI policy: layered assessment design, clear use guidelines, teacher judgement, and student AI literacy, not one rule or one tool.
•Safeguarding and inclusion lens: Designated Safeguarding Lead Training, AI and Online Safety for Designated Safeguarding Leads training (2026), and a SENCO qualification shape how I assess AI risk for multilingual, SEND, and neurodivergent students.
•AI literacy in the curriculum: digital and AI fluency built into Future Focused: The Future of Work.
•Networks: Digital Education Council (institutional affiliation through the NYU network) · Active Member, International AI in Education Society · Generative AI in Education, University of Glasgow (2026).

04 

Teaching philosophy

My overarching goal is psychological safety. Students participate more when they can ask questions, make mistakes, and take intellectual risks without embarrassment.

I believe teaching must be fundamentally collaborative, examining perceived hierarchies between instructors and students to create environments characterized by discovery, self-awareness, and belonging. The most important aspect of my work is whom I teach, not what I teach.

I was awarded a competitive Experiential Learning Curriculum Design Fellowship that let me build each course from blank page to fully operational. My pedagogy has a name: Students as Partners, an approach that treats students as collaborators in their own learning rather than recipients of it.

My curriculum experience spans five frameworks: IGCSE and A-Level (including A-Level at Harrow International School Shanghai), IB Diploma, English National Curriculum (adapted for international contexts), California state standards (adapted), and undergraduate liberal arts under Middle States Commission on Higher Education accreditation.

Respect

Every student arrives with a story I cannot see. My job is to figure out their present status and cook with whatever is on hand.

Reciprocity

Students review each other's work, negotiate roles in team projects, choose their own inquiry topics, and evaluate their own learning using structured DEIP reflections (Describe, Explain, Interpret, Plan).

Shared responsibility

Learning is not something I do to students. It is something we build together, from the first day's syllabus discussion to the final portfolio showcase.

The courses

Four interdisciplinary first-year courses at NYU Shanghai. Each uses a genuinely difficult question as the vehicle for inquiry, writing, and argument. Course-specific materials built around curated readings and sources rather than a prescribed textbook.

First-year course · Fall 2026 · Current

Future Focused: The Future of Work

Three guiding questions: What is work? How do we work now, and how might that change? How do we prepare for the future workplace?

A course I designed from blank page to full delivery, grounded in Future-Ready Competencies drawn from six international frameworks. Students build critical and creative thinking, self-direction, collaboration, empathy, ethical responsibility, citizenship, and digital fluency through curated readings, structured discussion, and source-based writing.

The course integrates a structured partnership with the Career Development Center: four visits, written reflections, a resume draft, and portfolio work.

Portfolio system: Working Portfolio → three formative Portfolio Builds with DEIP reflections (Describe, Explain, Interpret, Plan) → final Showcase Portfolio → oral Student Showcase.

Assessments: Two listening exams, informative presentation, persuasive speech, final portfolio with showcase, participation. View the course schedule and materials →

(Un)Sustainability

First-year course · EAP 101 · 2021 – 2025

Systems Thinking

Two connected questions: How do we grow as learners and thinkers? How does the world around us work?

This 101-level English for Academic Purposes (EAP) course helps you keep building the high-level language, communication, and critical thinking skills you need to succeed at an English-speaking university. At the 101 level, you will learn to facilitate and take part in seminar discussions with growing control: gaining the floor, building on peers' ideas, asking for clarification, and respectfully challenging assumptions.

This thematic seminar uses systems thinking to explore two connected questions: how we grow as learners and thinkers (how our habits, routines, and ways of seeing shape our choices over time), and how the world around us works. You will use four systems thinking tools: the Habits of a Systems Thinker (ways of approaching complex problems), the Iceberg Model (looking beneath events to the patterns and structures that cause them), causal loop diagrams (mapping how causes and effects feed back on each other), and system archetypes (recurring patterns such as Limits to Growth). You will start by mapping your own habits and mental models, then apply the same lens to community and global challenges.

You will lead and join midterm and final seminar discussions based on research from primary and secondary sources. You will also complete a multimedia video project in which you plan and conduct your own interview with someone outside the university, then analyze it alongside academic research.

Assessments: Participation, homework and Habit Cards journals, midterm seminar, final seminar, final video project. Course informed by the Waters Center for Systems Thinking.

First-year course · 2020 – 2021, 2023, 2026

The History of Rock and Roll

Big question: How did music both reflect and reshape American society?

A 12-week journey from Mamie Smith (1920) to Kendrick Lamar (2015), tracing how music shaped politics, economics, technology, race, gender, and identity.

Capstone: A research interview with someone outside NYU Shanghai, synthesized into a multimedia video presentation.

Assessments: Listening logs, course engagement, midterm seminar, final seminar, video project. Course informed by TeachRock.

The EAP Poster Show

I organized the EAP Poster Show every fall from 2018 through 2025, eight consecutive years. It is the only whole-department annual public event in English for Academic Purposes at NYU Shanghai.

A two-hour, university-wide exhibition where EAP students design original research posters on self-selected topics and give repeated 1–3 minute oral presentations to circulating visitors: students, faculty, and staff from across the university. Structured engagement requires presenters to visit peer posters and complete feedback forms. Students may collaborate across sections and courses.

Adapted to online delivery during COVID (2020–2021) with scheduled Zoom presentation slots and a digital poster gallery. Poster topics have ranged from deepfake technology and climate policy to Disney economics, string theory, and the history of money. The event has its own dedicated institutional budget and continues as a departmental tradition.

Debate & student voice

Debate and student voice run through my entire career. At Dulwich and Harrow I built the programs from scratch. The through-line starts at the University of Vermont, where Tuna Snider and the World Debate Institute taught me that competitive debate is the fastest way to teach a young person to think on their feet, argue with evidence, and hold a room.

Highlights: Debate Fellow at the Beihang World Debate Academy (2015); Teaching Fellow at the International Debate Academy Slovenia (IDAS, 2016), a 100-hour World Schools format academy in Kranjska Gora; and Advisor to NYU Shanghai's Collective Voice Debate.

The point was never debate for its own sake. It was building the muscle that lets students speak up, push back, and hold their ground with evidence, whether in a tournament, a seminar, or a boardroom.
