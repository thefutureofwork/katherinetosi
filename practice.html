How I teach and why

The Practice

I teach English for Academic Purposes at NYU Shanghai. But the real work is building classrooms where first-year students from seventy countries learn to think out loud, argue with evidence, and trust that the room will hold them while they try.

01 

Teaching philosophy

My overarching goal is psychological safety. Students feel comfortable expressing themselves, asking questions, and making mistakes without fear of embarrassment. This matters especially in language learning, where taking risks and making errors are part of the process. When students feel safe, they participate. When they participate, they learn.

I believe the focus of teaching must be fundamentally collaborative, examining perceived hierarchies between instructors and students to create environments characterized by discovery, self-awareness, and belonging. The most important aspect of my work is whom I teach, not what I teach.

I integrate digital technologies, interdisciplinary inquiry, and experiential learning into every course. Assignments build critical thinking, communication, collaboration, digital literacy, adaptability, and cross-cultural competence. My pedagogy has a name: Students as Partners.

Respect

Every student arrives with a story I cannot see. My job is to figure out their present status and cook with whatever is on hand.

Reciprocity

Students co-create syllabi, review each other's work, negotiate roles in team projects, and evaluate their own learning using structured DEIP reflections.

Shared responsibility

Learning is not something I do to students. It is something we build together, from the first day's syllabus discussion to the final portfolio showcase.

02 

Students as Partners in practice

SaP is not a label I attach after the fact. It is a sustained, targeted pedagogical strategy that shapes how I design assessments, structure class time, and share authority with learners.

•Peer review and feedback built into every project cycle
•Reflective journals using the DEIP framework (Describe, Evaluate, Interpret, Plan) require students to analyze their own learning strategies and group work
•Team-based SDG campaigns and poster shows require students to negotiate roles, set goals, and evaluate contributions
•The syllabus is updated based on class discussion and student feedback
•Self-directed learning assignments with peer and self-evaluation
•Student participation tracking sheets used collaboratively to discuss final grades

03 

The courses

I have designed four interdisciplinary first-year courses at NYU Shanghai. Each uses a genuinely difficult question as the vehicle for academic English: speaking, listening, source work, reflection, and the confidence to argue in a second language.

Fall 2026 · Current

Future Focused: The Future of Work

Big question: What will work look like in the future, and how can I prepare for it?

Five units: Understanding Work, Technology and the Changing Workplace, The Future Workplace, Ethics and Citizenship in the Age of AI, Communication Skills for the Future. Integrated Career Development Center partnership with four visits, resume drafting, career fair preparation, and a final portfolio showcase. All original course materials (no textbook). Student Workbook with ACAPS source evaluation and Cornell note frameworks.

Assessments: Two listening exams (20% each), informative presentation (20%), persuasive speech (20%), final portfolio with student showcase (10%), participation (10%).

2024 – 2025 · 38 students

(Un)Sustainability

Big question: What does sustainable development look like, and what is your role in it?

Students completed 4–5 hours of community volunteering, ran informative and persuasive SDG campaign presentations, wrote weekly DEIP reflective journals, used the MIT En-ROADS climate simulator in role-playing exercises, and presented at an end-of-semester poster show. Direct democracy voting systems created by students were shared with student government.

Previous · 20 students

Systems Thinking

Capstone: A 30-minute research interview with someone the student does not know, synthesized into a 15-minute video presentation using primary and secondary sources.

Weekly objectives included mapping causal loops, analyzing the Iceberg Model, and surfacing assumptions on controversial issues. Peer review and student-led presentations were core components.

05 

Leadership development

In November 2025 I was accepted into the National Professional Qualification for Headship at UCL Institute of Education, ranked number one in the world for education for the fourteenth consecutive year. My thesis examines how school leaders can guide their communities through AI and academic integrity: not with fear or blanket bans, but with ethical frameworks, redesigned assessments, and genuine collaboration with teachers and students.

In August 2025 I passed the School Leadership Licensure Assessment (SLLA 6990, score 164), qualifying me as a school administrator in grades 7–12 in the United States.

My Leadership Circle diagnostics confirm strengths in moral purpose, authenticity, composure, and decisiveness. My development focus is the Relating cluster: mentoring and developing others, fostering team play, and bringing people along rather than charging ahead alone.

My vision for education: schools and universities that are ethical, evidence-informed learning communities where young people grow as whole people, intellectually, socially, and morally, rather than places that simply produce exam results.
