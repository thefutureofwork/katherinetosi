How I teach and why

The Practice

I teach English for Academic Purposes at NYU Shanghai, where I have taught over 500 first-year students from 70+ countries since 2018. But the real work is building classrooms where first-year students from seventy countries learn to think out loud, argue with evidence, and trust that the room will hold them while they try.

01 

Teaching philosophy

My overarching goal is psychological safety. Students feel comfortable expressing themselves, asking questions, and making mistakes without fear of embarrassment. This matters especially in language learning, where taking risks and making errors are part of the process. When students feel safe, they participate. When they participate, they learn.

I believe the focus of teaching must be fundamentally collaborative, examining perceived hierarchies between instructors and students to create environments characterized by discovery, self-awareness, and belonging. The most important aspect of my work is whom I teach, not what I teach.

I was awarded a competitive Experiential Learning Curriculum Design Fellowship that let me build each course from blank page to fully operational. I integrate digital technologies, interdisciplinary inquiry, and experiential learning into every course. Assignments build critical thinking, communication, collaboration, digital literacy, adaptability, and cross-cultural competence. My pedagogy has a name: Students as Partners.

My curriculum experience spans five frameworks: IGCSE and A-level, IB Diploma, English National Curriculum (adapted for international contexts), California state standards (adapted), and liberal arts under Middle States Commission on Higher Education accreditation.

Respect

Every student arrives with a story I cannot see. My job is to figure out their present status and cook with whatever is on hand.

Reciprocity

Students co-create syllabi, review each other’s work, negotiate roles in team projects, and evaluate their own learning using structured DEIP reflections.

Shared responsibility

Learning is not something I do to students. It is something we build together, from the first day’s syllabus discussion to the final portfolio showcase.

02 

Students as Partners in practice

SaP is not a label I attach after the fact. It is a sustained, targeted pedagogical strategy that shapes how I design assessments, structure class time, and share authority with learners.

•Peer review and feedback built into every project cycle
•Reflective journals using the DEIP framework (Describe, Evaluate, Interpret, Plan) require students to analyze their own learning strategies and group work
•Team-based SDG campaigns and poster shows require students to negotiate roles, set goals, and evaluate contributions
•The syllabus is updated based on class discussion and student feedback
•Self-directed learning assignments with peer and self-evaluation
•Student participation tracking sheets used collaboratively to discuss final grades
•End-of-semester poster shows and showcases where students present their work to the wider university community
•Community partnerships embedded into coursework: students volunteer with Stepping Stones Shanghai (teaching English to migrant children) and Good-hope Bakery of Special Youth (supportive employment for young adults with neurodiversity)
•Direct democracy voting systems designed by students and shared with student government as working models
•As Campus Point of Contact for the UN Millennium Fellowship (2026–present), mentors students from coursework into funded social-impact projects: the classroom is the entry point, not the end of it

03 

The courses

Four interdisciplinary first-year courses at NYU Shanghai. EAP 100 builds presentation skills; EAP 101 builds seminar and research skills. Each uses a genuinely difficult question as the vehicle for academic English. All original course materials (no textbook).

EAP 100 · Fall 2026 · Current

Future Focused: The Future of Work

Big question: What will work look like in the future, and how can I prepare for it?

Five units: Understanding Work, Technology and the Changing Workplace, The Future Workplace, Ethics and Citizenship in the Age of AI, Communication Skills for the Future. Integrated Career Development Center partnership with four visits, resume drafting, career fair preparation, and a final portfolio showcase. Student Workbook with ACAPS source evaluation and Cornell note frameworks. CCD modules: Industry Exploration, Write Your First Winning Resume, Become an Interview Master Today. View the course schedule and materials →

Assessments: Two listening exams (20% each), informative presentation (20%), persuasive speech (20%), final portfolio with student showcase (10%), participation (10%).

EAP 100 · 2018 – 2025

(Un)Sustainability

Big question: What does sustainable development look like, and what is your role in it?

Students completed 4–5 hours of community volunteering with Stepping Stones Shanghai (teaching English to migrant children) and Good-hope Bakery of Special Youth (a public welfare bakery providing supportive employment for young adults with neurodiversity). Ran informative and persuasive SDG campaign presentations, wrote weekly DEIP reflective journals, used the MIT En-ROADS climate simulator in role-playing exercises (opening with a “life lottery” that assigned students to developed, developing, or underdeveloped country contexts reflecting global population distributions, then tasking groups with climate policy that addressed equity and tradeoffs), and presented at an end-of-semester poster show. Direct democracy voting systems created by students were shared with student government.

Course informed by Compass Education Sustainability Practitioners Certification (Licensed Tutor).

EAP 101 · 2021 – 2025

Systems Thinking

Capstone: A 30-minute research interview with someone the student does not know, synthesized into a 15-minute video presentation using primary and secondary sources.

Weekly objectives included mapping causal loops, analyzing the Iceberg Model, and surfacing assumptions on controversial issues. Peer review and student-led presentations were core components.

EAP 101 · 2020, 2026

The History of Rock and Roll

Big question: How did music both reflect and reshape American society?

A short survey of pre-rock and rock history exploring the main eras and key events within 20th and 21st century United States. Themes include politics, economics, technology, race and ethnicity as they relate to the songs students discover. Academic rigour and genuine delight are not opposites: students build listening comprehension, evidence-based argumentation, and academic writing through material they care about deeply.

05 

Leadership development

In November 2025 I was accepted into the National Professional Qualification for Headship (NPQH) at UCL Institute of Education, ranked number one in the world for education for the fourteenth consecutive year. The NPQH is the UK’s gold-standard leadership qualification for headteachers, recognized internationally as evidence of readiness to lead a school. My implementation project, AI and Academic Integrity, examines how school leaders can guide their communities through the challenges of generative AI: not with fear or blanket bans, but with ethical frameworks, redesigned assessments, and genuine collaboration with teachers and students. The project draws directly on my own work at NYU Shanghai, where I piloted an AI policy framework.

In August 2025 I passed the School Leadership Licensure Assessment (SLLA 6990, score 164), qualifying me as a school administrator in grades 7–12 in the United States. I also hold a Vermont English Language Arts Teaching License, Grades 7–12 (valid through June 2029).

Leadership diagnostics through the NPQH consistently identify moral purpose, authenticity, composure under pressure, and decisiveness as my core strengths. What I am deliberately building is patience with pace: bringing a whole staff along rather than moving at the speed of the people who already agree with me.

My vision for education: schools and universities that are ethical, evidence-informed learning communities where young people grow as whole people, intellectually, socially, and morally, rather than places that simply produce exam results.
