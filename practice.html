How I teach and why

The Practice

I teach English for Academic Purposes at NYU Shanghai, where I have taught over 500 first-year students from 70+ countries since 2018. But the real work is building classrooms where they learn to think out loud, argue with evidence, and trust that the room will hold them while they try.

01 

Teaching philosophy

My overarching goal is psychological safety. Students participate more when they can ask questions, make mistakes, and take intellectual risks without embarrassment.

I believe teaching must be fundamentally collaborative, examining perceived hierarchies between instructors and students to create environments characterized by discovery, self-awareness, and belonging. The most important aspect of my work is whom I teach, not what I teach.

I was awarded a competitive Experiential Learning Curriculum Design Fellowship that let me build each course from blank page to fully operational. My pedagogy has a name: Students as Partners, an approach that treats students as collaborators in their own learning rather than recipients of it.

My curriculum experience spans five frameworks: IGCSE and A-level, IB Diploma, English National Curriculum (adapted for international contexts), California state standards (adapted), and undergraduate liberal arts under Middle States Commission on Higher Education accreditation.

Respect

Every student arrives with a story I cannot see. My job is to figure out their present status and cook with whatever is on hand.

Reciprocity

Students review each other's work, negotiate roles in team projects, choose their own inquiry topics, and evaluate their own learning using structured DEIP reflections.

Shared responsibility

Learning is not something I do to students. It is something we build together, from the first day's syllabus discussion to the final portfolio showcase.

03 

The courses

Four interdisciplinary first-year courses at NYU Shanghai. Each uses a genuinely difficult question as the vehicle for academic English. All original course materials.

EAP 100 · Fall 2026 · Current

Future Focused: The Future of Work

Three guiding questions: What is work? How do we work now, and how might that change? How do we prepare for the future workplace?

A course I designed from blank page to full delivery, grounded in Future-Ready Competencies drawn from six international frameworks. Students build critical and creative thinking, self-direction, collaboration, empathy, ethical responsibility, citizenship, and digital fluency through original readings, structured discussion, and source-based writing.

The course integrates a structured partnership with the Career Development Center: four visits, written reflections, a resume draft, and portfolio work.

Portfolio system: Working Portfolio → three formative Portfolio Builds with DEIP reflections → final Showcase Portfolio → oral Student Showcase.

Assessments: Two listening exams, informative presentation, persuasive speech, final portfolio with showcase, participation. View the course schedule and materials →

EAP 100 · 2018 – 2025

(Un)Sustainability

Big question: What does sustainable development look like, and what is your role in it?

Students completed assessed community-engaged service with Shanghai non-profits, ran SDG campaign presentations, wrote DEIP reflective journals, used the MIT En-ROADS climate simulator in equity-focused role-playing exercises, and presented at an end-of-semester poster show. Student-designed direct democracy voting systems were shared with student government.

Course informed by the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals and the work of Jeffrey Sachs.

EAP 101 · 2021 – 2025

Systems Thinking

Big question: How do the systems around you actually work, and how can you improve them?

Built on the Waters Center for Systems Thinking framework: students progress through systems habits and tools, then apply system archetypes to real-world problems of their choosing.

Capstone: A 30-minute research interview with someone outside NYU Shanghai, synthesized into a multimedia video presentation.

Assessments: Participation, homework and journals, midterm seminar, final seminar, final video project.

EAP 101 · 2020, 2026

The History of Rock and Roll

Big question: How did music both reflect and reshape American society?

A 12-week journey from Mamie Smith (1920) to Kendrick Lamar (2015), tracing how music shaped politics, economics, technology, race, gender, and identity.

Capstone: A research interview with someone outside NYU Shanghai, synthesized into a multimedia video presentation.

Assessments: Listening logs, course engagement, midterm seminar, final seminar, video project. Course informed by TeachRock.

05 

Debate & student voice

Debate and student voice run through my entire career. At Dulwich and Harrow I built the programs from scratch. The through-line starts at the University of Vermont, where Tuna Snider and the World Debate Institute taught me that competitive debate is the fastest way to teach a young person to think on their feet, argue with evidence, and hold a room.

At Dulwich College Beijing I was appointed Debate Fellow at Beihang University and served as a teaching fellow at the International Debate Academy Slovenia (IDAS), the World Schools residential coaching program Tuna co-founded. I built Dulwich's competitive debate program from the ground up, organized and adjudicated World Schools format tournaments (including NSDA (National Speech and Debate Association) China National events and NHSDLC (National High School Debate League of China) Beijing Invitationals), and coached student teams across the region. At NYU Shanghai I am Faculty Advisor to Collective Voice Debate.

The point was never debate for its own sake. It was building the muscle that lets students speak up, push back, and hold their ground with evidence, whether in a tournament, a seminar, or a boardroom.

06 

Leadership development

In November 2025 I was accepted into the National Professional Qualification for Headship (NPQH) at UCL (University College London) Institute of Education. My implementation project, AI and Academic Integrity, examines how school leaders can guide their communities through the challenges of generative AI: not with fear or blanket bans, but with ethical frameworks, redesigned assessments, and genuine collaboration with teachers and students.

In August 2025 I passed the School Leadership Licensure Assessment (SLLA 6990) with a score of 164, the assessment US states use in licensing school administrators. I also hold a current Vermont English Language Arts Teaching License, Grades 7-12 (valid through June 2029).

Leadership diagnostics through the NPQH consistently identify moral purpose, authenticity, composure under pressure, and decisiveness as my core strengths. What I am deliberately building is patience with pace: bringing a whole staff along rather than moving at the speed of the people who already agree with me.

My vision for education: schools and universities that are ethical, evidence-informed learning communities where young people grow as whole people, intellectually, socially, and morally, rather than places that simply produce exam results.
